            <h1 class="imp-text-3xl imp-md:text-5xl imp-font-extrabold imp-text-white imp-mb-6 imp-leading-tight">Riverside Community Trust: Eliminating 78 Hours of Annual Data Entry</h1>
            <div class="imp-flex imp-items-center imp-justify-center imp-gap-6 imp-text-slate-400 imp-text-sm">

            <article>
                <p class="imp-text-xl imp-text-slate-600 imp-font-medium imp-mb-8">How a volunteer coordinator stopped rebuilding her list by hand, and what the duplicate records were really costing.</p>
                <div class="imp-note-box"><p>Illustrative example. Riverside Community Trust is a composite organisation built from patterns we see repeatedly in nonprofit Salesforce work. It is not a named customer, and the figures shown are representative of the category rather than measured at one client.</p></div>
                <h2>At a glance</h2>
                <div class="imp-article-table-wrap"><table class="imp-glance"><tbody><tr><th>Organisation</th><td>Riverside Community Trust, community services nonprofit</td></tr><tr><th>Salesforce</th><td>NPSP, 14 users</td></tr><tr><th>Volume</th><td>About 120 volunteer signups per month, spiking to 400 during campaigns</td></tr><tr><th>Problem</th><td>Manual reconciliation of Google Form signups into NPSP</td></tr><tr><th>Solution</th><td>Google Form Auto Sync plus a record-triggered Flow</td></tr><tr><th>Implementation</th><td>One afternoon, one admin</td></tr><tr><th>Annual time recovered</th><td><span class="imp-pill">About 78 hours</span></td></tr><tr><th>Recurring cost</th><td><span class="imp-pill">$0</span></td></tr></tbody></table></div>
                <h2>Executive summary</h2>
                <p>Riverside spent roughly 90 minutes each Friday transcribing volunteer signups from a Google Sheet into NPSP. The visible cost was coordinator time. The larger and less visible cost was a duplicate rate of approximately 7%, which fragmented volunteer service history and blocked a planned recognition programme. Automating capture removed the transcription work entirely and reduced duplicate creation to near zero. The recognition programme launched the following quarter.</p>
                <h2>What did the manual process actually cost?</h2>
                <p>Before recommending any change, the admin measured four weeks of the existing process. This is the step most organisations skip, and it is what makes the rest of the analysis possible.</p>
                <div class="imp-article-table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Measure</th><th>Baseline (4-week average)</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Signups received per week</td><td>About 30</td></tr><tr><td>Coordinator time per week on transcription</td><td>90 minutes</td></tr><tr><td>Time from signup to record in NPSP</td><td>2 to 5 days</td></tr><tr><td>Signups not entered before the shift</td><td>About 4 per month</td></tr><tr><td>New Contacts that duplicated an existing volunteer</td><td>About 8 per month</td></tr><tr><td>Estimated duplicate rate</td><td>About 7% of signups</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
                <p>Two figures are worth separating. Ninety minutes a week is 78 hours a year, which is the number that gets a project approved. The 7% duplicate rate is the number that actually mattered.</p>
                <h3>The compounding cost of duplicates</h3>
                <p>Each duplicate splits one volunteer's service history into two partial records. Over roughly two years of operation, Riverside had accumulated an estimated 200 duplicate Contacts.</p>
                <p>The consequence was not cosmetic. Riverside could not answer a question that should have been trivial: who are our most committed volunteers? The data existed but was fragmented across records, so any report on cumulative hours or shift count understated participation for exactly the volunteers who participated most.</p>
                <p>A planned volunteer recognition programme was postponed twice for this reason. A recognition programme that thanks the wrong people is worse than none at all.</p>
                <h2>Why did the standard fixes not fit?</h2>
                <figure><div class="imp-figure-panel"><img width="900" height="507" src="https://res.cloudinary.com/du42kbmmn/image/upload/w_900,f_auto,q_auto/v1789121157/04.2_fp5bzf.png" alt="Manual copy and paste process compared with an automated Google Forms to Salesforce flow" loading="lazy" class="imp-figure-img"></div><figcaption>The manual path on the left, the automated path on the right.</figcaption></figure>
                <p>The team evaluated three options before landing on a native app.</p>
                <h3>Automation platform</h3>
                <p>Quoted at approximately $1,200 per year at their volume. Two objections killed it.</p>
                <ul>
                    <li><strong>Budget framing.</strong> Recurring software spend in a nonprofit is defended against programme delivery. "Software to move data between two systems we already pay for" is a difficult sentence to say to a board.</li>
                    <li><strong>Data governance.</strong> Volunteer forms collect emergency contacts and accessibility needs. The operations director asked who else would process that data. The honest answer, a commercial automation vendor, triggered a vendor assessment, a processing agreement and a privacy notice change. The compliance effort outweighed the convenience.</li>
                </ul>
                <h3>Custom integration</h3>
                <p>Rejected on maintenance risk. Riverside has no developer, and the volunteer coordinator role had turned over twice in three years. Anything only one person understood would fail at the next handover.</p>
                <h3>Doing nothing</h3>
                <p>The status quo. Worth naming as an option because it was the incumbent and, without the baseline measurement above, would probably have won by default.</p>
                <h2>How was it implemented?</h2>
                <p>Total elapsed time: one afternoon, one admin, no developer.</p>
                <ol>
                    <li id="rv-step-1"><strong>Form inventory.</strong> One hour spent listing every active Google Form in the organisation. Eleven were found; seven were still in use; two had been collecting responses nobody had reviewed in months. This hour reshaped the rollout order.</li>
                    <li id="rv-step-2"><strong>Package install into sandbox.</strong> Google Form Auto Sync created its object, two permission sets and a setup tab.</li>
                    <li id="rv-step-3"><strong>Form connection.</strong> The five-step wizard generated the endpoint and credentials. The admin created an External Client App, set a dedicated Run-As user under the Policies tab, and pasted the generated script into the volunteer form's Apps Script editor. About 15 minutes.</li>
                    <li id="rv-step-4"><strong>Test submission.</strong> Appeared in Salesforce in approximately four seconds.</li>
                    <li id="rv-step-5"><strong>Routing Flow.</strong> The substantive work, and the part that required actual design thinking.</li>
                </ol>
                <h3>The routing logic</h3>
                <p>A record-triggered Flow on Form_Response__c, filtered to the volunteer form by form name:</p>
                <ul>
                    <li>Get Records on Contact, filtered by the submitted email address</li>
                    <li>Decision: match found, use the existing Contact; no match, create a Contact</li>
                    <li>Create the Volunteer Shift against the correct date</li>
                    <li>Write the created record ID back onto the Form Response for traceability</li>
                    <li>Fault path: mark the response as Failed with the error message</li>
                </ul>
                <p>The fault path is the element Riverside would tell other nonprofits not to omit. Under the manual process a failed entry produced no artifact at all. Now a failure leaves a queryable record with the original submission intact, available to correct and reprocess.</p>
                <h2>What were the results?</h2>
                <p>Measured over the three months following go-live, against the four-week baseline.</p>
                <div class="imp-article-table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Measure</th><th>Before</th><th>After</th><th>Change</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Coordinator time per week</td><td>90 min</td><td>Under 10 min</td><td><span class="imp-pill">-89%</span></td></tr><tr><td>Annual coordinator hours</td><td>About 78</td><td>About 8</td><td><span class="imp-pill">-70 hrs</span></td></tr><tr><td>Signup to record in NPSP</td><td>2 to 5 days</td><td>Seconds</td><td><span class="imp-pill">Over -99%</span></td></tr><tr><td>Duplicate Contacts per month</td><td>About 8</td><td>Under 1</td><td><span class="imp-pill">-88%</span></td></tr><tr><td>Signups missed before a shift</td><td>About 4 per month</td><td>0</td><td><span class="imp-pill">-100%</span></td></tr><tr><td>Recurring software cost</td><td>$1,200 quoted</td><td>$0</td><td><span class="imp-pill">-$1,200</span></td></tr></tbody></table></div>
                <h3>The result that was not on the table</h3>
                <p>The volunteer recognition programme launched the following quarter. For the first time the cumulative service history in NPSP was complete enough to trust, because new signups stopped fragmenting it and the existing duplicates could be merged against a stable baseline.</p>
                <p>That outcome is difficult to quantify and is the one the coordinator cites first.</p>
                <blockquote><p>"After two years of Fridays, watching the first test submission land in about four seconds looked faintly ridiculous."</p><cite>Volunteer Coordinator, Riverside Community Trust</cite></blockquote>
                <h2>Why did this work?</h2>
                <p><strong>The baseline measurement did the persuading.</strong> Four weeks of data converted an annoyance into a 78-hour figure and a 7% error rate. Neither was arguable.</p>
                <p><strong>Capture and routing were separated.</strong> Because submissions land on a neutral object before any Flow runs, a routing error never destroys data. During the first three weeks two Flow bugs were found and fixed, and in both cases the affected submissions were reprocessed from the preserved raw records.</p>
                <p><strong>One form was perfected before scaling.</strong> The volunteer form ran alone for three weeks. Both bugs above surfaced in that window, where diagnosing them meant examining one Flow rather than five.</p>
                <p><strong>The logic lives where the next admin will look.</strong> Matching rules sit in Flow Builder. In a sector with high turnover, that is a durability property, not a convenience.</p>
                <h2>What would we do differently?</h2>
                <p>Two things, offered because a case study where everything went smoothly is not useful to anyone planning the same project.</p>
                <p><strong>Deduplicate the existing backlog first.</strong> Riverside automated capture, then cleaned the 200 historical duplicates. Doing it in the reverse order would have been easier, because merges would not have been competing with new inbound records.</p>
                <p><strong>Add form-side validation earlier.</strong> Both Flow failures traced to malformed email addresses. Enabling response validation on the email question in Google Forms, a two-minute change, would have prevented both.</p>
                <p>The same capture-then-route pattern is covered step by step in our guide to <a href="/blog/google-form-response-to-salesforce-lead-flow.html">routing form responses with Flow</a>, and the nonprofit context in more depth in <a href="/blog/google-forms-to-salesforce-nonprofits-npsp.html">Google Forms to Salesforce for nonprofits</a>.</p>
